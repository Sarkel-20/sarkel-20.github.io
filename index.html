My method

On a broker account you optimize your equity curve. At a prop firm the rules decide who gets paid, so I optimize something else: the odds and the speed of getting paid, in net dollars per trading day.

An account is an option

You pay a small fee for the right to lose the firm's money and keep a share of what's above a threshold. That contract has a value of its own, before any skill. I price it: trailing floors and their lock, consistency caps, daily limits, minimum days, payout buffers and caps, splits, eval prices, resets, activation fees.

Every state has a value

An account isn't just its balance. Distance to the floor, where the trailing stops, days traded, payouts already taken: together they're the state. For each state the model computes what the account is still worth in expected dollars: its residual value. That's what tells you when to push, when to protect, when to let it go.

Risk depends on the state

The right risk per trade and reward-to-risk aren't fixed. Close to the floor, far from it, just before a payout, just after one: each calls for a different size. Exact dynamic programming (backward induction over the account's states) finds that policy, Monte Carlo checks it.

Eval and funded are two different games

In an eval you're buying a pass: hit the target before the floor, at the lowest cost per pass. Funded, you're extracting cash before the floor catches you, under payout and consistency rules. Same trader, different objective, different optimal risk.

Costs and chaining included

A blown eval gets re-bought, a funded account lost before its first payout sends you back to an eval. The model runs the whole chain with every fee in it and ranks plans by expected net dollars per trading day, computed as total over total, not an average of ratios.

Zero edge first

Everything starts from a coin-flip trader: what do the rules alone give you? Edge is added on top, never assumed. If a plan only works with a big edge, it doesn't work.

Checked, not fitted

Every optimum is re-measured on fresh paths. An independent simulator (a million paths per case) and a second exact solver cross-check the model. A hard invariant: at zero edge, it can't create money from nothing. Rules are read from each firm's site by two independent passes; when a rule is ambiguous, the model takes the most pessimistic reading.

What it doesn't model

A firm refusing to pay, a firm going under, taxes. Those are judgment calls, and I make them separately.
